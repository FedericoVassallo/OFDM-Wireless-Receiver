- (explanation of behaviour of channel 2)
	The fundamental cause of the failure is that Viterbi algorithm relies on a feedback loop where the phase estimate of the previous symbol is used as the reference for the 	current symbol. Channel 2 introduces a continuous phase rotation over time. This means the true phase is a moving target that your tracker must constantly chase. As long as the error between your tracker's estimate and the true phase remains small, the system works. However, the moment this error exceeds 45 degrees (the decision boundary for QPSK), the estimator snaps to the wrong quadrant. Since the algorithm blindly trusts its previous estimate, this error becomes permanent, and the constellation begins to spin uncontrollably, forming the ring shape observed in your plots.

	The reason this failure happens at different times for different alpha values is distinct. When you use a low alpha like 0.01, the filter is extremely stiff and relies almost 	entirely on past measurements. It simply cannot update its estimate fast enough to keep up with the rapid rotation of the channel. A lag error accumulates linearly with every 	symbol, and by the 10th symbol, this lag exceeds the 45-degree safety margin, causing the tracker to lose sight of the signal purely due to its own sluggishness.

	Conversely, when you use a high alpha like 0.99, the filter is highly responsive and easily keeps up with the rotation, which is why it survives much longer, up to around 40 	symbols. However, because it has almost no memory to average out incoming data, it becomes extremely sensitive to noise. Eventually, a random spike of Gaussian noise will push a 	single symbol across the quadrant boundary. Because the filter is so aggressive, it accepts this noise spike as the truth, causing an immediate Cycle Slip. Therefore, the failure 	is inevitable: you either fail early because you are too slow to track the rotation, or you fail later because you are too sensitive to noise to maintain stability.
